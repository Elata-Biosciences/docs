## Contact PPG

**Photoplethysmography (PPG)** shines light into the skin and measures how much
comes back. As blood volume pulses with each heartbeat, the reflected light
changes. It's the same principle as rPPG, but with a sensor touching the skin,
which gives a much stronger signal. Smartwatches use it, and so do some
headbands, such as Muse S.

The Elata SDK's `ppg-web` package reads a headband's optical sensor over Web
Bluetooth and estimates heart rate and heart-rate variability on the device.

---

## Heart-rate variability (HRV)

Your heart doesn't beat like a metronome. The time between beats varies
slightly from beat to beat, and that variation is **heart-rate variability**.
Higher HRV at rest is often linked with recovery and relaxation, and lower HRV
with stress or fatigue, though it also varies with age, fitness, breathing,
and time of day.

The SDK reports two common time-domain measures:

| Measure | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **RMSSD** | Root mean square of successive differences between beats. Reflects short-term, beat-to-beat variation. |
| **SDNN** | Standard deviation of the beat-to-beat intervals over the recording. Reflects overall variation. |

Strictly, variability measured from PPG is **pulse-rate variability**. It
closely tracks ECG-based HRV at rest, but the two aren't always
interchangeable.

---

## What makes HRV meaningful

| Condition | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **At rest** | Agreement between wearable and ECG-based HRV is very good to excellent at rest, and declines as activity increases |
| **Long enough** | A minute or more of clean data; a few seconds isn't enough |
| **Clean beat detection** | One missed or extra beat distorts RMSSD badly, so noisy segments must be excluded |
| **Compared to yourself** | HRV differs widely between people; trends against your own baseline are more informative than absolute numbers |

The SDK withholds HRV values (returns no value) when it can't detect beats
reliably, instead of reporting a misleading number.

---

## What it can't claim

PPG-based heart rate is widely used and generally accurate at rest. It does
not diagnose arrhythmias or other heart conditions: published reviews note that
ECG confirmation remains necessary for diagnosis.
